Fly Circuit Anatomy

The model follows light through the fly's eye, one 20-millisecond step at a time. Eight drawings of the machine, in the order the signal travels — then three of what we measured.

Why this matters

Models like this one are how the field now reads mechanism off a connectome: wire the network exactly as the fly is wired, train it on a visual task, then look inside and say this is how the fly computes motion. A 2024 Nature paper did exactly that with the model drawn on this page and predicted the circuit for direction selectivity in T4 cells.

In April 2026 a reproducibility study retrained it fifty times, changing only the random seed. Every model reached the same task performance — validation loss varied by 1.4% — and they did not agree on the mechanism. Individual learned parameters varied by 20% to over 100% across seeds; 418 of the 604 synaptic magnitudes were exactly zero in some seeds and not in others. The connectome pins the wiring, but not what the wiring does. A mechanism read from one trained model is one draw from a large family.

The hypothesis: the connectome is not the only biology we know. Neurons of a type are excitatory or inhibitory, not both (Dale's law); parameters are shared within a cell type; and a real neuron's time constant depends on its own state. Arm B adds those three constraints to arm A. If they are enough to make different seeds converge on one mechanism — with no loss of task performance — then mechanistic claims from such models become identifiable, and the constraints are the thing that earns that trust.

One neuron is a leaky voltage

Each neuron has a single number, its voltage. Every 20 ms it is pulled toward a target — its resting bias plus the weighted, rectified voltages of everything that synapses onto it, plus light if it is a photoreceptor — and it gets part of the way there, set by its time constant τ. What it sends onward is its rectified voltage.

Update, exact
v ← e−dt/τ·v + (1−e−dt/τ)·u

The two arms differ in exactly two places

Same wiring, same neuron, same 929 trainable numbers, same starting point. Arm B adds two biological constraints; arm A carries inert placeholders so the parameter count matches.

Arm A baseline

Signs as the connectome reports them. Four source types (R8, Am, C2, C3) excite some targets and inhibit others.

τ is a learned constant per cell type, the same for every input.

τ = τt

Arm B constrained

Dale's law: one sign per source type, from the published neurotransmitter. Flips 10 of the 604 pairs, 8,590 synapses.

τ listens to the neuron: three gates per type let its own voltage and drive stretch or shrink τ, bounded between τ/e and τ·e.

τ = τt · exp(tanh(gv·v + gu·u + gb))

Reading motion off the circuit

The voltages of 34 output types — the T4/T5 motion detectors and the Tm and TmY cells feeding them — are stacked as 34 channels over the hex grid and passed through a small hexagonal convolutional decoder that predicts optic flow: one motion arrow per column. The network firing

Put together, this is what runs when an edge crosses the eye. Photoreceptors report luminance; lamina cells answer only to the change; two medulla pathways carry it on, one fast and one about 250 ms slower; a T4c cell multiplies the fast signal from its own column with the delayed signal from the column below. When the edge moves up, the two arrive together and T4c fires. When it moves down, they miss each other and T4c stays quiet. Which medulla types supply the fast and the slow arm is exactly what the fingerprint reads off a trained model — and what the 50 released models disagree about.

Sixty thousand training steps, replayed

Arm A trained for a quarter of flyvis's schedule with a checkpoint every thousand steps.
